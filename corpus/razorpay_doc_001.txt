The Razorpay engineering interview is centered on the design of a payment gateway end to end, since that is the core of what Razorpay's engineering organization builds and operates at scale. Candidates should expect the system design round to start from a prompt like "design a payment gateway that can process a transaction between a customer and a merchant" and to be pushed through increasingly specific failure scenarios from there.

A payment gateway design interview at Razorpay typically probes several themes in depth. First, idempotency of payment requests: candidates should be able to explain why a client might retry a payment request due to a timeout or network failure, and how the system guarantees that a retried request does not result in the customer being charged twice, usually via an idempotency key supplied by the client and a deduplication table keyed on that value. Second, handling webhooks: since payment status often changes asynchronously, with a bank or card network confirming a transaction after the initial API call has already returned, candidates should be able to describe a reliable webhook delivery and retry system, including how to handle out-of-order or duplicate webhook deliveries.

Reconciliation is a third recurring theme: how does the system detect and correct discrepancies between what Razorpay believes happened, its internal ledger, and what actually happened at the bank or card network, especially after partial failures. Candidates are expected to reason about ensuring exactly-once semantics in money movement — not "exactly-once delivery" in the messaging-system sense, but the stronger guarantee that a given payment intent results in exactly one net movement of money, even across retries, partial failures, and asynchronous confirmations. A strong answer usually combines idempotency keys, a durable ledger with strict debit and credit invariants, and a reconciliation job that compares the ledger against upstream settlement reports on a schedule.

Candidates are also assessed on API design for payments, including versioning, how errors are surfaced to merchants in a way that is actionable by distinguishing a retryable network error from a permanent decline, and how sensitive fields like card numbers are kept out of logs and out of Razorpay's own systems where possible through tokenization. Database modelling for transactions is another focus area: candidates should be able to design a schema that makes it structurally difficult to double-count money, typically through append-only ledger entries rather than mutable balance fields.

Handling failure and retries more generally is woven through the whole interview: what happens if the database write succeeds but the response to the client is lost, what happens if a downstream bank call times out and you do not know if it succeeded, and how the system distinguishes a transient failure worth retrying from a permanent one. Candidates who can name the specific failure mode and the specific mechanism that closes it, rather than a general "add retries" answer, tend to do well.

A related area that comes up often is rate limiting and abuse prevention at the API layer, since a public payment API is a natural target for automated fraud attempts. Interviewers may ask how you would detect a burst of failed authorization attempts against the same card and throttle or flag it without blocking legitimate retries from the same merchant. Strong candidates connect this back to the idempotency discussion, noting that a well-designed idempotency key scheme also limits how much damage a naive retry storm can do.

Beyond system design, expect a coding round focused on correctness under concurrency, for example implementing a rate limiter or a small ledger with strict invariants under simulated concurrent access, and a behavioral round that explores how the candidate has handled high-stakes production incidents, since payment infrastructure has an unusually low tolerance for silent bugs. Candidates who can walk through a real incident using a clear timeline — detection, mitigation, root cause, and the follow-up fix — tend to leave a stronger impression than those who only describe what the bug was.

A theme that separates strong candidates from average ones is comfort with the settlement side of payments, not just the authorization side. Many candidates can describe how a card charge gets authorized in real time, but far fewer can explain what happens over the following days as funds actually move between the customer's bank, the card network, the acquiring bank, and finally into the merchant's account. Interviewers sometimes probe this deliberately by asking what should happen if a transaction is authorized successfully but the corresponding settlement never arrives, since that gap is exactly where reconciliation logic earns its keep, and a candidate who has only thought about the authorization path will often not have a good answer ready.

Multi-currency and multi-region considerations also come up in more senior interviews, particularly around how exchange-rate volatility interacts with the idempotency and ledger guarantees already discussed. If a payment is authorized in one currency but settled in another days later, the system needs a clear policy for which exchange rate applies and how that choice is recorded immutably in the ledger, so that reconciliation does not mistake a legitimate exchange-rate difference for a bug. Candidates who bring this up unprompted, even briefly, signal that they are thinking about the problem at the level Razorpay actually operates at rather than a simplified single-currency toy version of it.

Finally, interviewers pay close attention to how candidates communicate trade-offs rather than searching for a single "correct" architecture. Two candidates can propose meaningfully different designs for the same prompt and both score well, provided each can clearly articulate what their design optimizes for and what it deliberately sacrifices. A design that prioritizes low latency at the cost of a slightly more complex reconciliation process is a defensible choice; what interviewers penalize is a candidate who is unaware they have made that trade-off at all.
