CRED's technical interviews for engineering roles are built around distributed systems fundamentals, because CRED's core payments and rewards infrastructure is a distributed system end to end. Candidates should expect at least one round entirely dedicated to this area, and interviewers routinely probe follow-up questions two or three levels deeper than the textbook answer, so surface-level familiarity is not enough.

The centerpiece of the distributed systems round is consensus algorithms. Candidates should be prepared to explain how systems like Raft and Paxos work in detail: how a leader is elected, how log entries are replicated and committed, what happens during a network partition, and how the system recovers once the partition heals. Interviewers frequently ask you to reason about consensus algorithms from first principles rather than recite definitions — for example, "why does Raft require a majority quorum, and what breaks if you allow a minority to commit?" is a common follow-up. Being able to whiteboard a leader election timeline, including term numbers and split-vote scenarios, is a strong signal.

Beyond consensus itself, the round covers fault tolerance more broadly: how replication factor and quorum reads and writes trade off availability against consistency, how systems detect and handle node failures, and how retries interact with idempotency to avoid double-processing, a theme that recurs across CRED's payments-adjacent systems. Candidates should be comfortable discussing the CAP theorem not as trivia but as a lens for justifying real design decisions — when CRED's own systems favor consistency over availability, and why.

The interview also covers core data structures and system design more broadly. Expect questions on choosing between hash indexes and B-tree indexes for different access patterns, how a rate limiter is implemented at scale using a token bucket or a sliding window, and how to design a system that must remain correct under duplicate or out-of-order message delivery. System design questions typically start from a deceptively simple prompt, such as "design a notification service" or "design a ledger," and the bar is less about landing on one correct architecture and more about methodically working through requirements, bottlenecks, and trade-offs out loud.

A recurring theme across both the algorithms and system design rounds is eventual consistency versus strong consistency in distributed databases. Candidates should be able to give concrete examples of where each is appropriate: a user's account balance typically demands strong consistency, since you cannot show a stale number and let someone double-spend, while an activity feed or a "last seen" timestamp can tolerate eventual consistency in exchange for lower latency and higher availability. Being able to name the mechanism, not just the label, matters — for example, explaining how vector clocks or last-write-wins resolve conflicting writes under eventual consistency.

Coding rounds at CRED lean toward practical problems over pure algorithmic puzzles: parsing and validating structured data, implementing a small in-memory cache with eviction, or simulating a simplified ledger with debit and credit invariants. Interviewers care about correctness under edge cases such as empty input, concurrent updates, and overflow, as much as the happy path. Candidates are also expected to talk through the time and space complexity of their solution unprompted, and to identify at least one edge case before being asked.

A common mistake candidates make is treating the system design round as a checklist exercise, naming components like "load balancer" and "cache" without justifying why each one is needed for the specific requirements given. Interviewers score much more highly for candidates who tie every component back to a stated constraint, such as "we need a cache here specifically because read volume is a hundred times write volume," rather than adding components by reflex. Another common mistake is skipping failure-mode analysis entirely — a strong answer always spends time on what happens when a dependency is slow or down, not only on the happy path.

Finally, candidates should come prepared with questions of their own about how CRED's engineering teams operate day to day, including on-call rotations, how incidents are reviewed, and how architectural decisions get made, since the later rounds are as much about mutual fit as technical depth.

Preparation for the distributed systems round tends to pay off most when it is grounded in a small number of systems studied deeply rather than a wide survey of many systems studied shallowly. Candidates who have implemented a toy version of Raft, even a simplified single-node simulation of leader election and log replication, consistently perform better than candidates who have only read about it, because the interview rewards the ability to reason about edge cases such as a leader that becomes partitioned but does not yet know it has lost quorum. Similarly, candidates who have worked through the classic two-generals and FLP impossibility results, even informally, tend to give sharper answers about why certain guarantees are fundamentally unattainable in an asynchronous network, rather than assuming a clever enough algorithm could eventually solve them.

On the system design side, a useful habit is to explicitly state assumptions about scale before diving into an architecture, since two designs that look similar on a whiteboard can have very different trade-offs depending on whether the system needs to handle a thousand requests per second or a million. Interviewers will often adjust the scale mid-interview specifically to see whether the candidate's design adapts gracefully or falls over, so treating the initial numbers as fixed rather than a starting point for negotiation is a common source of a weaker score. Candidates are also encouraged to sketch data models early, since a surprising number of system design interviews are effectively decided by whether the schema can represent the required invariants at all, independent of how well the surrounding services are described.

Time management within the round matters as well. Spending the first several minutes clarifying functional and non-functional requirements before writing anything on the board is consistently rewarded, since it signals that the candidate treats requirements gathering as part of the engineering process rather than an obstacle to get through before the "real" design work starts. Candidates who jump straight to naming technologies, such as immediately proposing a specific message queue or database without first establishing why that category of tool is needed, tend to be marked down even if the eventual choice turns out to be reasonable, because the interview is assessing the reasoning process at least as much as the final answer.
